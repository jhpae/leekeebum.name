Search the stacks

Give the Annex a melody and it will tell you where that melody has always been shelved. Short monophonic lines (up to 48 notes) are stored literally: the address encodes the notes, so opening it plays your melody back exactly. Longer or polyphonic material is reduced to its top voice first.

On the arrangement of this Annex

Borges imagined a library holding every book that 410 pages of an alphabet permit. This annex holds sound instead of letters. An address — hexagon : wall : shelf : volume : page — is hashed into a deterministic pseudo‑random stream, and that stream is read as a score: tempo, metre, key, mode, instrumentation, form, motifs, and every note. The same address always yields the identical file, byte for byte. Nothing is stored; nothing is random at read time.

Durations are drawn logarithmically from the interval [1s, 600s] and clamped to whole bars, so every page lasts at least one second and never more than ten minutes.

Reversibility. Search is not a lookup. A melody of up to 48 notes is encoded — pitch and quantised duration, base‑1024 — into a single integer, rendered in base‑36 as the hexagon's name and prefixed with x. Opening that address decodes the integer back into the same notes, then dresses them with accompaniment derived from the wall, shelf, volume and page. Your melody was always there; the search merely computes the shelf mark.

Everything runs in this one file: the score generator, a Standard MIDI File writer (format 1, 480 ticks per quarter), a MIDI parser for search, and a small oscillator synthesiser on the Web Audio API. No frameworks, no fonts, no network requests — once loaded, it works with the aeroplane switch on.
